Briefing · District procurement data

How the District buys software

Every technology contract the Office of Contracting and Procurement currently publishes, read and sorted by what the District is actually paying for.

Data pulled 23–24 August 2026 3,983 contracts · 114,255 purchase orders · 866,823 payments contracts.ocp.dc.gov

The District signs very few contracts to build software. It signs a great many to hire people and to open ordering channels, and most of the technology money moves through those.

Blind spot
The portal has no record type for task orders. Schedule vehicles carry the largest ceilings in this data, so the biggest single channel of technology buying cannot be followed. Some of it resurfaces as purchase orders or later contracts, but nothing links those back to their vehicle.

$1.2 billion authorised for hiring people, $38 million for building software

Sorting contracts by what they actually buy means reading them. The District's commodity codes will not do it: they are assigned by the buyer and applied loosely, so class 918, "consulting services", holds Medicaid managed care contracts worth billions of dollars, and class 920, "data processing, computer, programming and software services", holds a plumbing contractor's $14M contract. Every contract below was sorted from its title, vendor and awarding agency instead.

1,372 contracts classified as technology, $4,641M in ceilings. Notebook §"What the District is buying".

Building software is the smallest line. Staff augmentation carries thirty times its ceilings; ordering vehicles carry nearly fifty times. And $38M is a floor in a specific sense: it counts contracts structured as building to specification. Custom work bought as hourly labor, or through task orders this data cannot see, is not in it.

Half the technology ceiling authority sits on one schedule nobody can see through

The largest line in that table needs explaining, because it is not a purchase at all. An ordering vehicle is a contract that authorises future orders without buying anything. The District awards one to a pool of pre-qualified vendors, and agencies then place task orders against it as needs come up.

Almost all of them are one programme. Of 328 ordering vehicles, 305 are DC Supply Schedule contracts, awarded in near-identical form to a set of small vendors under titles like "DCSS — Information Technology Equipment and Software". Counting every technology contract that names DCSS, the programme accounts for 397 contracts and $2.4 billion, which is 52% of all technology ceilings. Those dollars are authority, not spending — a pool of near-identical ceilings that will never collectively be drawn — and they mix two things: $1.6B of vehicle ceilings, and $844M of DCSS-named awards that buy actual work, 75 of them staff augmentation.

Notebook §"Half the technology ceiling authority sits on one schedule".

The step in the middle is where the actual buying decision is made, and it is the one step the District does not publish. Everything after this point describes what can be seen around that gap.

The gap starts before the award, too. Tracing each of the 1,372 technology contracts to a posted solicitation — through the solicitation number its award document cites, or a close title match — finds one for 295, carrying 11.4% of the ceiling dollars; correcting for what the matching misses, no more than about a third of contracts and a tenth of the dollars ever pass through the portal's solicitations module. The big money arrives citing a 2007 schedule award or a GSA schedule instead. The module also hides its own solicitation numbers: 96% of rows display an internal document ID where the number should be. And the portal's own archive shows postings falling from 1,351 in FY2016 to 749 in FY2025.

$3.1 billion authorised over five years, $527 million paid

A contract amount is an authorisation. Comparing it against payments the District actually recorded shows how far apart the two are, and how differently contract types behave.

Both columns below cover the same five fiscal years, which matters more than it sounds. The payments table reaches back to FY2017 and contract option periods run out to 2031, so a careless comparison counts payments made before a ceiling existed against authorisation nobody could have drawn yet.

Option periods falling entirely inside FY2021–FY2025, against payments made in those same years. Recomputed by verify_claims.py §"authorised against paid"; the whole-history version is Notebook §"Ceilings are not spending".

Read those percentages as floors. Payments join to contracts by contract number, and a task order issued under an ordering vehicle is paid under the task order's own number, so it never joins back to the parent. Only 44% of technology contracts have any linked payment at all. And the portal keeps only surviving option periods — a finished contract's ceiling shrinks while its payments remain, which is how one system's $110M build shows a $7.3M record below.

Ten vendors take half the traceable technology money

Of $579M paid against technology contracts between FY2021 and FY2025, spread across 295 vendors, half went to ten of them. One took a sixth on its own. That $579M counts only the payments that carry a matching contract number, and the unlinked rest — including everything bought through the Supply Schedule — could be more or less concentrated. The top spot does not depend on the join, though. Purchase orders, a separate path into the data, also put Computer Aid first.

What the largest vendor's contract actually sells

Computer Aid's contracts are coded NIGP 962, "miscellaneous services, not otherwise classified", and the purchase orders written against them are coded 918, "consulting". Neither is a technology code, so an analysis filtered to IT commodity classes misses the District's largest technology vendor completely.

One of those contracts is titled "Information Technology Staff Augmentation Services", and its price schedule says plainly what is for sale. The $65M ceiling splits into $45M of "Labor Hour Type" and $20M of "Fixed Price Project", followed by a rate card: 45 job categories, four skill levels each, a not-to-exceed fully burdened hourly rate for every combination.

180 rates in all, $24.01 to $173.71, median $69.97, re-read from the cached award PDF by verify_claims.py.

Compare FAST Enterprises, which builds tax and benefits systems: $51M across 11 purchase orders, median $3.5M, priced against the system delivered rather than the hour.

What an hour of a person costs, where the District publishes it

Computer Aid's card is not the only one. 51 of the 434 technology contracts with a readable award document publish an hourly rate card — 2,471 not-to-exceed rates in all, from $24.01 for entry-level customer support to $1,015 for one vendor's IT subject matter expert, 325 of them read out of scanned awards the OCR recovery opened up. For 1,629 of those rates the match between job title and price was re-verified against the document text mechanically. The rest sit in documents whose PDF layout scrambles the columns, so their cards appear below as price ranges only, never as title-to-price pairs.

The staffing firms and small consultancies publish their rates. The biggest names mostly do not: the text layer of Accenture's award PDF on its DCAS oversight contract is 41 characters long, and KPMG's largest contract and Ridge IT's Zero Trust award have no award document attached at all. The vendors billing the most are the ones with no published prices.

Against what the federal government pays

GSA publishes the awarded ceiling rates on its own schedules — the same fully burdened, not-to-exceed idea, often the same job titles. Matching DC's categories to exact federal titles gives 119 comparable rates. The staff augmentation card runs at half of federal: across the 44 matched rates on Computer Aid's card, the median sits 50% below the federal median, and DC's top-of-scale Master rates are frequently beneath the federal entry median. Everything else scatters around federal, and the consultants sit well above it — Trusted Advisory Group's program managers bill 71–89% over the federal median, LimnoTech's project managers 77–83% over.

The hiring side of the same hour

The portal shows what gets billed; job ads show what the person is offered. The staffing firms recruiting for District seats post in the contract's own vocabulary — "Journeyman", "Senior", "Master" are the CW91684 tier names — and DC postings carry pay. Three captured on 24 August 2026 line up against the card directly:

Postings captured 24 August 2026; screenshots, raw pages and archive links in evidence/job_ads/. A posting using the tier vocabulary is not proof it fills a seat on this contract; the OCTO one names the agency outright.

The first two rows show the ordinary shape: the worker is offered roughly 70–78% of the billed rate, before whoever employs them takes taxes and overhead out of the rest. The third row shows the other pattern: on contract terms, at the top tier, the offer sits a dollar or two under the ceiling — the recruiting firm's cut lives somewhere other than the hourly spread.

One federal filing prices both sides of the same hour. Changing Technologies attested $55.00 an hour for a "Data Surveillance Project Manager II" placed at DC Health; its own published card prices a Project Manager at $160.08 to $166.54. The card rate is a fully burdened ceiling and the attested wage is a floor, so the gap holds taxes, benefits, overhead and margin at once — but both numbers are hourly, from the same company, on the public record.

Around the edges, the same hunt found: Cúram staffing ads — the skill DCAS runs on — offering $80–90 an hour, and one deleted posting titled "DHCF DCAS Business Analyst" at $98–108; D2Sol, holder of the $84M DCAS operations contract, hiring junior US staff and Bangalore staff through its own site with no pay posted, while its 2025 H-1B filings put Cúram developers at $130,000–$190,000 in the DC suburbs; FAST Enterprises offering $91,000–$96,000 for the implementation consultants who run systems like MyTax DC; and Computer Aid's own careers board carrying exactly one DC posting — the $90,000–$110,000 account manager who runs the staff-augmentation program. The billed workforce is recruited under other companies' names.

How many people is that? The data never says. It records dollars and rates, not heads. The arithmetic: Computer Aid runs about $93M a year in purchase orders; at its card's median $69.97 an hour that is roughly 1.3 million hours, or about 640 people working full time. Count only the payments that link back to its contracts and the floor is about 140. Nothing published narrows it further.

Eighty-six of ninety-five build contracts fix the scope before work starts

Award documents were read for how delivery is organised: what the price schedule commits to and what the deliverables schedule requires. 288 award documents carry a readable text layer; another 75 were scans or extraction failures, recovered by running the pages through a vision model, which brings the readable set to 363 of 476.

Read from award documents by the pass in delivery_config.yaml; counts recomputed by verify_claims.py §"delivery structure". Notebook §"Agile language".

Waterfall is the largest structure among contracts where a delivery method applies at all, and it is waterfall in the strict sense: 86 of the 95 fix their scope in advance, so changing what gets built requires modifying the contract. Incremental arrangements, where a team can be pointed at something different next month without a modification, account for 43.

Eleven of the 95 also promise to work in sprints

Fourteen contracts in the readable set commit to agile delivery at all — and eleven of them are structurally sequential and gated, saying so in the same document that fixes the scope and sets the acceptance gates. About a quarter of everything else is gated. Two of the fourteen are hand-read corrections of classifier misses, recorded with reasons in delivery_overrides.yaml; three more surfaced in the OCR-recovered scans, including the DC.gov redesign, which commits to Scrum by name. Counted separately, and excluded from the fourteen above, are 15 contracts where agile appears only as a hiring requirement: "Experience in Agile methodology such as Scrum, Kanban, or Scaled Agile Framework" sitting in a labor category description. That is a specification for the people being bought, and says nothing about how the work will run.

The District names a system in one contract out of five

273 of 1,372 technology contracts name a specific system in the title, so every count here is a floor. The names give the clearest picture of what the money maintains, and the pattern is that a handful of large systems absorb repeated contracts over years.

Notebook §"Named systems".

DCAS is the clearest case of how the District sustains a large system: at least 28 separate contracts across the years in this data, for operations and maintenance, application development teams, licences, hosting, printing and mailing. No single contract builds it. It is kept running by a rolling series of awards, most of them for people and services rather than for software.

One system from start to today: what DCAS cost

The DC Access System decides who gets Medicaid, SNAP and TANF in the District. It began as the ACA build: a 2012 solicitation, won by Infosys Public Services at $49.5 million, three planned releases, most of it paid with enhanced federal matching funds. The payments table only reaches back to FY2017, so the original build predates the portal's records.

Release 3 — the hard part, non-MAGI Medicaid and the integrated portal — was built by KPMG under a contract titled simply "DCAS R3". The portal's surviving record of that contract is one option period at $7.3 million. The payments show $50 million paid citing the contract's number across FY2019–22, plus another $60 million of KPMG payments at Health Care Finance carrying no contract number at all — and DCAS R3 is KPMG's only contract at that agency. The portal keeps only surviving option periods, so a finished contract's record shrinks while its payments remain. Accenture was separately contracted to oversee the build.

Since 2025 the running of it is visible again: D2Sol invoices exactly $3,490,537.83 a month — $41.9 million a year — as the steady-state price before Cúram licences, the mobile app, AWS resale, printing, or call centers. Assembling every contract that can be defensibly tied to the system finds 49, with $249M in visible ceilings and $284M in attributable payments; 19 of the 49 show no payments at all, and 7 show payments above their surviving ceiling.

The workforce shows up one more way. H-1B wage filings are public, and the federal versions name the client agency outright. eSystems Inc — a vendor whose entire portal presence is two purchase-order rows — holds seven certified filings whose placement field reads "DC ACCESS SYSTEM ADMINISTRATION (DCAS), DEPARTMENT OF HEALTH CARE FINANCE (DHCF)" — five of them senior Cúram developer team leads at $170,000–$203,000. Thirty-three of D2Sol's name DHCF. Others name OCTO, the Department of Employment Services, and the Office of the Attorney General. One certified filing covers a Cúram developer working for DHCF from a home in Cary, North Carolina, at $129,999. In all, 110 Cúram-titled filings carry a Washington DC worksite, from six employers; two of the six appear nowhere in the portal, in any table.

Asked at FY2024 budget oversight whether DCAS was working as intended, the Deputy Mayor replied with an emphatic no — the Council's word. The District was sued in 2017 over an 86.34% SNAP timely-processing rate; by FY2022 the rate had fallen below half that, and in April 2023 the federal Food and Nutrition Service rejected the District's corrective plan as inadequate. An Inspector General audit of Medicaid eligibility is the closest thing to an accounting; no DC Auditor report on the system exists.

Where the reading is incomplete

Delivery structure could be read for 363 of 476 candidate contracts: 288 with a native text layer, 75 recovered from scans and extraction failures. The remaining 113 have no award document attached at all.

Payments join to a contract for 44% of technology contracts. Of 866,823 payment rows, 478,716 carry no contract number at all, covering $17.8B of real spending that cannot be attributed to any contract.

Contracts were sorted by a language model reading titles, vendors and agencies, not contract text. Scored against 20 hand-read cases picked to be hard: 20 of 20 on what is bought, 20 of 20 on delivery structure, 18 of 20 on what agile language is doing — the two misses are genuine commitments it filed as incidental, corrected by hand as described above. Twenty cases bound the error loosely. Treat category totals as approximate; the ordering between categories is what survives.

Questions this will raise

The ones an incoming administration should ask, answered here where the data can answer them, and marked plainly where it cannot.

How much does the District spend on technology in a year?

Nobody can say from published data. Payments traceable to technology contracts run $58M in FY2021 rising to $175M in FY2025 — read those as floors, because only 44% of technology contracts have payments that link to them at all, and $17.8B of payments across every category carry no contract number. Any single annual figure would be an estimate built on the visible minority.

Is anything here improper?

Nothing on this page alleges misconduct, and none of it should be read that way. Ordering vehicles, staff augmentation, fixed-scope delivery and loosely applied commodity codes are the process working as designed. The findings are about what that process makes visible and what its contracts structurally commit to — not about anyone breaking rules.

Do the unpublished task orders actually exist, or would publishing them mean new paperwork?

They exist as numbered records; the payment ledger shows it. Payments made between FY2021 and FY2025 reference 3,079 distinct CW-numbered awards, and 1,471 of them — carrying $0.8B — appear nowhere in the portal's published contract list. Some may have been published once and removed since. Either way, money moves today under award numbers that no reader of the portal can look up, so making them visible is a disclosure decision, not a record-keeping project.

Is Computer Aid overpaid? Are these vendors any good?

This data cannot say, in either direction. It shows what was authorised, what rates were agreed and what was paid — not what was delivered, whether it worked, or what the same work costs elsewhere. The rate card is reproduced above so the agreed prices are at least visible; judging them needs comparators this data does not contain, and nothing in it measures performance.

Why does it matter that contracts say agile and run waterfall?

Because the contract binds and the method language does not. A contract that fixes scope, sequences deliverables and gates payment on acceptance stays waterfall whatever method its statement of work names: changing what gets built requires a contract modification. The teams inside can hold every ceremony they like, but the thing the District can change its mind about was set at award. Whether that is the right way to buy software is a policy choice this data does not make. It only shows which choice is actually written down.

Would another city look different?

Unknown; no comparison was attempted, and most large governments also buy technology heavily through staffing and schedule vehicles. What can be compared today is disclosure: the federal government publishes its task orders on USAspending, and the District does not publish its own.

What would close the gaps?

Each item maps to a defect documented in the repository. Publish task orders placed under schedule vehicles. Require a contract number on every payment row — 55% have none. Fix the fiscal-year filter that silently returns everything, and the payment field that repeats a check-run total on every row in the run. Standardise vendor names. Attach award documents at all — 113 of 476 technology build contracts have none. These are publishing changes to systems that already hold the records.

Can this be rerun after the transition?

Yes — anyone can, without asking us. make pull fetches a fresh snapshot, make classify re-sorts it, make verify recomputes every figure on this page and fails loudly if one no longer holds, and make test re-scores the classifiers against hand-read contracts. The portal is live, so the counts will have moved a little, but the method holds.
