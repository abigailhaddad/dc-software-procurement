# Does a DC solicitation title name the vendor that wins it?

Generated by `analysis/solicitation_names_winner.py`. Every count below is
recomputed from the files in `data/`; nothing is transcribed by hand except
the adjudications and the brand list, both of which are in the script.

## The rate

Of the **82** contract/solicitation pairs where the award document itself
prints the solicitation number, the solicitation title contains the winning
vendor's name in **1**.

- `CW52586` — Quickbase, Inc — solicitation "FY17-T0O- Quickbase"

8 of the 82 pairs (6 distinct vendors) have no distinctive token
after the stoplist is applied and cannot be detected by this method at all: ADVANCED NETWORK CONSULTING, Computer Solution LLC, EC America, INC., Public Consulting Group, Inc., SZ PM Consultants, Inc, Tech Analysis, Inc..
Read strictly the rate is 1 of 82; read as a share of vendors that
could have been detected it is 1 of 74. Neither is
a meaningful number on its own — see the base rate.

## The base rate

Three ways of asking how often a vendor name shows up in a solicitation title
when it is not the winner's.

**Other companies in the same 82 titles.** Matching all 3,449 registered DC
suppliers plus every technology contract vendor against the same 82 titles
produces 30 matches on companies that did not win. Reading each one
by hand, 24 are ordinary English colliding with a company name ("Arbor E & T"
against "Arbor Pravail", "E-Plan, Inc" against the word plan) and
6 are real:

- `CW101465` — "FY22 Dell Mobile Precision Workstation" — Dell is the manufacturer of the workstation being bought
- `CW115955` — "FY21-BMC Software Maintenance" — BMC Software is the maintained product's maker; MVS Inc won
- `CW129708` — "DOB - Accela Maintenance and Enhancement" — Accela Inc makes the permitting system; Carahsoft won
- `CW87709` — "Cisco Firewall Hardware" — Cisco Systems makes the firewall; n2grate won
- `CW89121` — "FY21-OCTO-FileNet License Renewal and Maintenance Support" — FileNet Corporation makes the licensed product; Document Systems won
- `CW92388` — "FY21 FileNet Support Services" — FileNet Corporation makes the supported product; Document Systems won

So 6 of the 82 solicitation titles name a company that did not win,
against 1 that name the company that did. Every one of the 6 is a
software or hardware manufacturer whose product the District was buying
through somebody else.

**Chance.** Shuffling the winning vendors across the same 82 titles 2,000 times
gives a mean of 0.013 hits per shuffle, and at least one hit in
1.3% of shuffles. One observed hit is more than chance would give,
but one is one.

**The whole archive.** Run unadjudicated against all 16,245 solicitations in
the pull, the matcher fires on 6,437 titles (40%). That is an upper
bound made mostly of the same false positives, and it is the point: strings
shaped like company names are ordinary in DC solicitation titles. One title in
82 naming the actual winner is not an elevated rate against that background.

## Control: the matcher is not broken

Contract titles are written after award, so they can name the winner and often
do. Running the identical matcher over them:

- all 1,383 technology contracts: 468 contract titles name their own vendor (34%; 109 vendors unmatchable)
- the same 82 pairs: 4 contract titles name the vendor, against 1 solicitation title

The matcher finds vendor names in the hundreds where they are present. The
near-zero solicitation rate is the data, not the method.

The pattern is brand-name buying resold through an intermediary, not
solicitations written around the firm that wins them. A title saying
"FY21-OCTO-FileNet License Renewal" narrows the product to one vendor's
software; it does not narrow the bidder, and the bidder that won was a
reseller. Whether specifying the product is itself a competition problem is a
different question from the one this script asks, and needs the justification
documents rather than the titles.

## The competition cross

`data/competition/contract_competition.csv` holds 70 of these 82 contracts.
Its flags come from reading determination-and-findings attachments, and among
those 70 the coverage is not_sampled 53, ocr_readable 1, read 16.
Flags raised: limits_competition 5, avoids_dc_market 3, single_source_language 3, exempt_from_competition 2, software_lock_in_cite 0.

The overlap is one contract against 5 flagged for limiting competition, and it
is empty — the one solicitation that named its winner is not among the
flagged. There is no cross to report. A 1-by-5 table cannot be
disproportionate in either direction, and with 53 of the 70 never sampled
the flagged set is mostly missing data anyway.

## The title-matched population, reported separately

The other 224 traced contracts are paired to solicitations by title
similarity. That pairing is circular for this question: a solicitation whose
title contains a vendor name is more likely to be matched to a contract whose
title contains the same name. The rate below is inflated by construction and
must not be pooled with the 82.

- `CW48527` — NICE SYSTEMS INC. — solicitation "NICE Software Upgrade & Expansion" (similarity 1.0)
- `CW49629` — COMPUWARE CORPORATION — solicitation "Compuware Software Maintenance" (similarity 1.0)
- `CW77381` — Avaya, Inc. — solicitation "Avaya Software Maintenance - GSA" (similarity 0.839)
- `CW89194` — MICROSOFT CORPORATION — solicitation "Microsoft Premier Support Services" (similarity 1.0)
- `CW89782` — Dell — solicitation "FY21 - DELL VDI VXRail Infrastructure" (similarity 1.0)
- `CW92433` — Kony Inc — solicitation "FY21 - KONY Software License and Cloud Subscription" (similarity 1.0)

6 of 224. All 6 are cases where the manufacturer itself holds the
contract, and in 5 of the 6 the contract title and the solicitation title
normalise to the same string, which is what produced the match. NICE Systems is
here — the case that prompted this question — and it is the same brand-name pattern:
the title names NICE's software, and NICE sells it directly.

## Answer

1 of 82. Solicitation titles named a company that lost more often (6 of 82)
than one that won. There is no finding here about buys steered to a named
bidder. The measurable thing in these titles is brand-name specification,
which is in 28 of 82 of them and is a different claim requiring different
evidence.
